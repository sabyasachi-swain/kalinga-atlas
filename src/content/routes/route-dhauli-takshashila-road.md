Ashoka carved a rule at Dhauli. It said an officer must also come from Takshashila, a city near modern Pakistan. This inspection had to happen at least every three years, after a first visit within five. It is one of the longest journeys named in any Kalinga inscription. [Hultzsch, 1925, Hultzsch, Corpus Inscriptionum Indicarum I (1925), First Separate Rock Edict, clause BB, p. 97; identification pp. xxxvii-xxxviii]

Scholars agree Takshashila and Kalinga were part of one Mauryan government. A prince governed the northwest, at Taxila, alongside governors at Ujjain in the west and Tosali in Kalinga. [Kulke, 1998, 3rd edn (Routledge, 1998), p. 67]

## For scholars

This edict orders a real travel schedule. It is not just a mention of two places. But no source maps the road itself. The line shown, about 2,600 km long, is schematic. This is the longest route in the whole atlas. The Dhauli copy speaks to officials at Tosali. The same rule appears on a Jaugada copy too. That one speaks to officials at Samapa instead. So that route is recorded on its own. Scholars link Takshashila to a place called Shahdheri, near Rawalpindi. That is the standard guess. It is not checked directly here. [Hultzsch, 1925, Hultzsch, Corpus Inscriptionum Indicarum I (1925), First Separate Rock Edict, clause BB, p. 97; identification pp. xxxvii-xxxviii]
