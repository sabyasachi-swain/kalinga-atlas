Palur was a special spot on the coast. Here, ships stopped following the shore. They turned out into the open sea, heading for Southeast Asia. Archaeologists found Indian pottery buried at Sembiran, on the north coast of Bali. It was exactly the kind of pottery made near Kalinga. [Patra, n.d., p.109; Ardika, 1991, p.221]

Diggers at Sembiran found more than pottery. They found rouletted ware. They found a broken piece like pottery made at Arikamedu. They even found a scratched letter, in an old script called Kharoshthi. These lay in dirt about two thousand years old. [Ardika, 1991, p.225]

A Greek mapmaker named Ptolemy wrote about this coast. He named a point where ships left the shore. From there, they sailed into open sea, toward a storied land he called "Khryse." Some historians think this point lay near Palur. [Patra, 2014, p.121]

A festival in Odisha still remembers voyages like these. Every year, at Cuttack, people mark Kartika Purnima, the full-moon day of the month of Kartika. A story says this was the day Sadhaba merchants once sailed for Bali, Java, Sumatra, Borneo and Sri Lanka. People float tiny paper boats on the water. Each boat carries a betel leaf, an areca nut and a small lit lamp. [Nanda, 2019, pp.41-42]

## For scholars

Ardika and Bellwood call the Sembiran pottery "Indian." They do not say it came from Kalinga. Two separate Odisha writers make that link instead, based only on matching pots. [Ardika, 1991, p.232; Nanda, 2019, p.44] Ptolemy's point of departure aimed toward Khryse. Scholars think that was the Malay Peninsula, not Bali itself. Different scholars even disagree on where, along this coast, that point of departure lay. [Patra, 2014, p.121]

The festival is a living memory, not a written record. It cannot date the old voyages, or prove they happened as the story says. The route's real evidence is the Sembiran pottery, not the festival. [Nanda, 2019, p.41]
