Ashoka's rule from Dhauli was carved again at Jaugada. It said an officer had to come from Takshashila too. Takshashila sits near modern Pakistan. This shows how far Ashoka's officials had to travel. [Hultzsch, 1925, Hultzsch, Corpus Inscriptionum Indicarum I (1925), First Separate Rock Edict, clause BB, p. 97 (Jaugada copy pp. 111-116); identification pp. xxxvii-xxxviii]

Scholars agree Takshashila and Kalinga were run by the same government. A prince governed the northwest, at Taxila. Other princes governed Ujjain, in the west, and Tosali, in Kalinga. [Kulke, 1998, 3rd edn (Routledge, 1998), p. 67]

## For scholars

This edict orders a real travel schedule. It does not describe the road itself. So the line shown here is schematic. The Jaugada copy of this rule is addressed to officials at Samapa, not Tosali. So it is kept apart from the Dhauli-to-Takshashila route. This is the longest land route in the atlas, apart from that one. [Hultzsch, 1925, Hultzsch, Corpus Inscriptionum Indicarum I (1925), First Separate Rock Edict, clause BB, p. 97 (Jaugada copy pp. 111-116); identification pp. xxxvii-xxxviii]
